Designers were among the first professionals asked to work with, and to design for, generative AI. My work in this area asks two questions: how should designers build generative AI applications, and how can AI take part in the creative work that designers do?

## Design principles for generative AI

Generative AI applications pose new design challenges: their outputs vary, they can be wrong, and they can create harm. I led a cross-IBM effort to distill research on human-AI collaboration and co-creation into **design principles** and concrete strategies for designing generative AI applications. This work was used to train over 3,000 IBM design practitioners, and it was later extended into a handbook chapter.

## AI in group ideation

Designers often run group ideation sessions, but these sessions can stall, or they can lack a particular expertise or viewpoint. We built two prototypes to explore how an LLM-based agent might participate:

- **Koala** is a Slack bot that joins group brainstorming discussions. It contributes ideas either reactively (when mentioned) or proactively (when it judges it has something valuable to add). All participants preferred having Koala in their groups. Koala contributed most of the ideas generated, including many of the ideas the groups rated best. Most top ideas were shaped by both people and the AI.
- **Collaborative Canvas** is a shared virtual canvas where people and an AI place digital stickies together. Design practitioners saw value in AI for filling expertise gaps and breaking through lulls. They also suggested that AI could act as a facilitator, not only as a participant.

From these experiences, we developed a framework for designing an AI agent's **participatory behaviors** in group conversations: when it should contribute, what and where, and who decides.
